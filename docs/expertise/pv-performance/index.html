Expertise

PV performance and technology choice

Which modules, mounting structures, and trackers give the lowest cost of electricity at a given site, and how climate, albedo, and dust change the answer.

Solar photovoltaic (PV) module datasheets are rated at standard test conditions that rarely occur in the field. Temperature, spectrum, ground reflectance (albedo), and dust all change how much energy a system produces, so the cheapest technology on paper is not always the best choice for a site. My work compares technologies on their energy yield and cost of electricity at real locations.

What I have worked on

  • Bifacial modules and trackers worldwide. A global modelling study of fixed-tilt, single-axis, and dual-axis systems with monofacial and bifacial modules found that bifacial modules on single-axis trackers would increase energy yield by 35% on average and give the lowest levelised cost of electricity (LCOE) on 93.1% of the world's land area. Dual-axis trackers would produce the most energy, but their cost is still too high for them to be cost-effective. An earlier study of 55 locations showed that whether bifacial modules pay off on fixed structures depends on latitude and albedo: below 40° latitude, they need a minimum albedo of about 0.12 to 0.30, depending on the location.
  • How trackers should track. Following the best orientation rather than the sun would gain less than 1.8% in irradiance below 60° latitude, so the extra sensors may be hard to justify at the latitudes of Southeast Asia and the Middle East.
  • Test conditions that match the climate. With colleagues at the Solar Energy Research Institute of Singapore (SERIS), I derived climate-specific reference conditions from long-term weather data at four test sites, in Singapore, Australia, Germany, and China. They differ noticeably from standard test conditions, and the technology that yields the most energy depends on the local climate.
  • Soiling. With colleagues at the National University of Singapore (NUS), I worked on estimating soiling losses from surveillance-camera images using deep learning, including a version that runs on low-cost edge devices at the site. Dust and other deposits can reduce output by up to 50%.
  • Agrivoltaics in arid climates. A review with colleagues at the Qatar Environment and Energy Research Institute (QEERI) estimated the agrivoltaic potential of cultivated arid and semi-arid land at 139 to 231 TWp; deployment on even 1% of that land could deliver 1.3 to 2.3 TWp.
  • Cell and module design. Optimisation of the metal contact design of solar cells and modules to raise efficiency, reduce silver use, and suit the conditions where the modules will be installed.
  • Performance in desert conditions. At QEERI, I work on PV, battery storage, and grid technologies in desert conditions, and I contributed to the International Energy Agency Photovoltaic Power Systems Programme (IEA PVPS) Task 13 report on optimising PV systems for different climates (2025).

Where I have applied it

Technology selection and energy yield assessment are part of every feasibility study I have worked on, from floating PV in the Philippines, Sri Lanka, and Suriname to ground-mounted and rooftop PV for developers and investors. I have also analysed the operating performance of more than 6 MWp of floating and ground-mounted PV systems with storage. More on my experience.

Questions I help answer

  • Monofacial or bifacial modules, fixed tilt or trackers: which gives the lowest LCOE at this site?
  • How far will real output depart from datasheet ratings in this climate?
  • How much energy does soiling cost at this site, and how can it be monitored?
  • Which yield assumptions should a lender accept for a technology with little local track record?
