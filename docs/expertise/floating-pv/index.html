Expertise

Floating solar PV

Where floating PV makes sense, what it costs compared with ground-mounted PV, and how to take a reservoir, lagoon, or coastal site from screening to feasibility.

Floating photovoltaics (floating PV) places solar modules on reservoirs, lakes, ponds, and, increasingly, near-shore and offshore waters. It suits places where land is scarce, expensive, or contested, and water bodies that already have a grid connection, such as hydropower reservoirs. Yet floating PV still typically costs more upfront than ground-mounted PV, and no system has yet reached its expected 25-year life, so project owners and lenders need site-specific evidence before they commit.

What I have worked on

  • Global market data. At the Solar Energy Research Institute of Singapore (SERIS), I maintained the SERIS Global Floating PV Database from 2022 to 2026: about 1,700 projects, over 10 GWp, in 66 countries. It records capacity, cost, float supplier, water body type, and coverage ratio, among other parameters, and it underpins the work below.
  • Design, cost, and risks. With colleagues, I reviewed how floating PV systems are designed, built, and operated. Installed capacity passed 9.6 GWp in 2024, and median capital expenditure fell to USD 1.25/Wp. Levelised cost of electricity (LCOE) generally remains higher than for land-based PV, but water conservation, synergies with hydropower and aquaculture, and less land-use conflict add value. The main open issues are operations and maintenance in harsher conditions, environmental impacts, and regulatory uncertainty for developers.
  • How much could be built on inland water. Covering 10% of the area of 249,717 inland reservoirs could host up to 22 TWp, enough to meet global electricity consumption and up to 5% of the world's water demand through avoided evaporation. Trackers could raise the specific yield of a typical 10° fixed-tilt system by up to 50% within ±40° latitude, and bifacial modules could add up to 4.5%.
  • Offshore floating PV. A global assessment of territorial waters and exclusive economic zones found that up to 95% of the offshore regions analysed fall within conditions suitable for current floating PV technology. In 84% of regions, less than 2% of the available offshore area would meet current electricity demand, and in 53% of regions that area comes at an LCOE below 15 US cents/kWh. Southeast Asia, particularly Indonesia and Malaysia, emerged as an early deployment zone, owing to its irradiance, bathymetry, and competitive LCOE.
  • Floating PV with hydropower. Of about 70 hydropower plants in Ecuador, 11 had favourable conditions for large-scale floating PV. Floating PV can add generation and reduce evaporation from the reservoir, which can help keep hydropower output up in dry seasons, when irradiance is typically higher.
  • Carbon finance. For a carbon finance programme in Indonesia, through the Deutsche Gesellschaft für Internationale Zusammenarbeit (GIZ), I estimated the LCOE of floating PV at candidate reservoir sites and the carbon price needed to close the gap between that cost and Indonesia's ceiling tariff for solar power.
  • Yield, reliability, and maintenance. As a contributor to the International Energy Agency Photovoltaic Power Systems Programme (IEA PVPS) Task 13, I worked on its 2025 review of floating PV energy yield, reliability, and maintenance. Yield models for floating PV still lack reliable data on module temperature, wave-induced losses, soiling (including bird droppings), and long-term performance loss, and weather databases often leave out coastal and offshore sites. Cables, connectors, and junction boxes may be more critical than the modules, because they may face more stress on water than on land. Field data on failures remain scarce, and there is no accelerated stress test designed for floating PV yet. Maintenance often needs divers or marine engineers, so remote monitoring, drone inspection, and automation could reduce its cost.

Where I have applied it

As the floating PV expert on teams led by Dornier Suntrace and Trama TecnoAmbiental, I worked on pre-feasibility, feasibility, and options studies funded by the Asian Development Bank (ADB) in the Philippines, the Inter-American Development Bank (IDB) in Suriname, the United States Agency for International Development (USAID) in Sri Lanka (30 to 50 MWp), and the World Bank in Mexico, Tuvalu (a lagoon site), and Viet Nam (floating PV with hydropower). The work covered technology selection, energy yield assessment, layout and preliminary engineering design, economic viability, grid interconnection and permitting, and implementation and ownership models. Questions I help answer

  • Which reservoirs, lakes, lagoons, or coastal sites are suitable, and how much capacity could each host?
  • How does the cost of floating PV compare with ground-mounted PV at this site, and what drives the difference?
  • What changes in design, energy yield, and maintenance when PV sits on water?
  • How can floating PV be combined with an existing hydropower plant?
  • What carbon price or concessional finance would make a floating PV project viable under a tariff ceiling?
  • Under what conditions could offshore floating PV compete in a given market?
